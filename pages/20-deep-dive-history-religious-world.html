Deep Dive — History & Religious World

UNESCO places the construction of the principal Elephanta caves broadly in the middle of the fifth to sixth centuries CE. IGNCA material discusses a widely held scholarly association with an early Kalachuri ruler in the third quarter of the sixth century and connects the early Kalachuris with the Lakulisha-Pashupata tradition of Shaivism.

Keep that wording in mind.

Widely held.

Not proven beyond doubt.

There is a temptation when visiting an ancient monument to want one famous king, one date and one dramatic story.

Archaeology is usually less tidy.

The exact patronage of Elephanta remains uncertain.

The evidence is much stronger for the artistic and religious context than for the identity of the person who ordered every excavation.

And that uncertainty is not a weakness.

It is part of the historical story.

The people who physically carved these images are largely anonymous.

Think about that the next time you stand before Sadashiva.

A seven-metre-high masterpiece survives.

Its creators do not have famous biographies.

We do not know which individual sculptor carved which face.

We do not know the names of the people who shaped every pillar.

But their decisions survive.

Proportion survives.

Gesture survives.

Expression survives.

Composition survives.

Skill survives.

The mountain has preserved their work even though it did not preserve their names.

There is something strangely appropriate about that.

The artists created a monument designed to make Shiva enormous.

They did not create a monument designed to make themselves famous.

One of the most useful names for understanding the religious background is Lakulisha.

Lakulisha is traditionally regarded as an important early teacher of the Pashupata school of Shaivism. IGNCA material connects the early Kalachuris with the Lakulisha-Pashupata tradition and specifically highlights the importance of the Yogishvara image.

Do not take that as evidence that Lakulisha personally built Elephanta.

There is no secure basis for that claim.

Instead, think of Lakulisha as a window into the religious environment.

Pashupata traditions emphasized Shiva devotion and disciplined religious practice. The yogic Shiva therefore has a much deeper significance than simply being a man sitting cross-legged.

He represents mastery.

Discipline.

Concentration.

Withdrawal.

Control.

And now compare that with Nataraja.

The yogi is still.

The dancer moves.

The same deity is being presented through completely different physical states.

That is one of the central ideas of the cave.
