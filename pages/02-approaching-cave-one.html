ELEPHANTA GUIDE
STOP 02

Approaching Cave One

You are approaching Cave One.

Before entering, stop.

If you can, stand where you have a broad view of the entrance.

Look at the proportions.

Look at the openings.

Look at the relationship between the exterior and interior.

The cave is not an accidental hole in the hill.

It was conceived as a temple.

UNESCO describes the main body of the cave as approximately twenty-seven metres square, supported by rows of six columns each, with the whole cave extending roughly thirty-nine metres from the front entrance to the back.

The plan also has a strong relationship to other Indian rock-cut temples, including Dumar Lena at Ellora.

That connection is worth remembering if you have visited Ellora.

Elephanta and Ellora belong to a larger history of Indian rock-cut architecture.

But Elephanta has its own identity.

Here, the sculptural program devoted to Shiva is unusually concentrated.

The cave becomes almost a three-dimensional theology.

Now walk inside.

As you enter, let your eyes adjust.

Don't immediately search for the famous face.

Look at the room.

Look at the pillars.

Look at the floor.

Look at the ceiling.

The cave's power comes partly from scale.

The hall is large enough to make you feel small.

But not so large that you lose the feeling of enclosure.

That balance matters.

The mountain is outside you.

The cave is around you.

And the sculptures are integrated into the walls.

Now imagine the ancient interior.

There was no modern electric lighting.

Natural light entered through the openings.

Different times of day would have created different patterns of illumination.

The sculptures would not have looked exactly the same at ten in the morning and three in the afternoon.

Shadows would move.

Surfaces would disappear and reappear.

A face could seem dramatically more powerful when half of it was in shadow.

So the cave was not merely a static visual display.

It was an environment.

Before we walk around the sculptures, a little context will make everything easier.

The cave is devoted primarily to Shiva.

But Shiva is not presented as a single character with one personality.

Shaivism contains many ways of understanding Shiva.

He can be the supreme deity.

He can be the ascetic.

He can be the cosmic dancer.

He can be the husband of Parvati.

He can be the destructive force that defeats demons.

He can be associated with fertility and generative power through the linga.

He can embody the union of masculine and feminine principles.

The Elephanta sculptors took this rich religious world and turned it into architecture.

One of the religious traditions relevant to the site's interpretation is the Pashupata tradition of Shaivism.

IGNCA scholarship discusses a connection between the early Kalachuris and the Lakulisha-Pashupata tradition and points to the importance of the Yogishvara image in this context.

Lakulisha is traditionally regarded as a major early teacher of the Pashupata tradition and, in later religious understanding, as a manifestation of Shiva.

But again, keep the distinction clear.

A religious connection is not proof that Lakulisha himself commissioned the cave.

We don't know that.

What we can say is that the cave belongs to a sophisticated Shaiva religious environment in which yoga, ritual, ascetic practice and multiple manifestations of Shiva mattered.

Now we can start walking.
